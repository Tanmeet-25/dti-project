A model that scores 0.98 and knows almost nothing

We trained drug–target interaction models on 289,282 measured binding pairs from BindingDB, then tested the same model four ways. On a random split it looks excellent. On genuinely new drugs and proteins, most of that performance turns out to have been recognition, not prediction.

The problem

Random splits reward memorisation

Split 289,282 drug–protein pairs at random and the same drug appears on both sides of the split, paired with different proteins. So does the same protein. A model can score well by learning which proteins are usually hit and which compounds are usually potent, without learning anything about how a drug and a protein interact.

The shape of the data makes this easy. There are 176,283 distinct compounds but only about 3,250 proteins, so each protein recurs in many pairs while most compounds appear once.

Compounds176,283 71.4% appear in exactly one pair
Proteins3,253 median 3 pairs, mean 89
One-sided targets423 of 1,053 well-tested proteins are >90% or <10% binders
Strong binders72.0% so accuracy alone is meaningless
Consequence. For 423 proteins a model can be right most of the time by recognising the protein and ignoring the drug entirely. We measured exactly that: a baseline that predicts each protein’s historical hit rate, never looking at the compound, reaches 0.848 ROC-AUC on the random split.

What the model actually uses

Remove the protein and it gets better

To find out which half of the input carried the signal, we retrained the same forest twice: once on the 2,048 compound fingerprint bits alone, once on the 420 protein composition features alone.

On unseen proteins the compound-only model scores 0.866 against the full model’s 0.745. Protein features help only while the protein is familiar. Once it is new, they actively mislead the trees, which have learned to route each pair by protein identity.

That is the project’s central result. What looks like learned interaction is largely compound-level bias: published compounds are usually optimised binders, so a model can guess from the molecule alone.

Why these choices

Ki and Kd measure binding directly, while IC50 depends on assay conditions. The two-order-of-magnitude gap between the classes removes borderline labels that would otherwise be noise.

Negatives are the subtle part. Drawing them from untested combinations is common and wrong: an untested pair may simply never have been assayed. Every negative here is a compound measured against that protein and found weak.

134 UniProt IDs map to more than one sequence — isoforms and mutants. Grouping by sequence would let a mutant of a test protein sit in training, so splits 3 and 4 group by UniProt ID instead.

Seeds matter more than confidence intervals here. Bootstrap intervals cover test-set sampling only. Re-running split 4 under three seeds moved the score by 0.035 — wider than those intervals — so we treat any difference under about 0.04 as unproven.
Limitations

What this does not show

  • “Unseen” means a different identifier, not new chemistry. Median similarity from a test compound to its closest training neighbour is 0.79, and 12% of split-2 test pairs have a near-duplicate above 0.95. A scaffold split would be stricter.
  • Composition features are a weak description of a protein. Amino-acid and dipeptide frequencies carry no pocket geometry and no residue order. Nearly every protein pair scores above 0.9 cosine similarity, which is why our nearest-protein baseline sat at chance.
  • Only one number is seed-checked. Split 4’s Random Forest score has three seeds behind it. Everything else is a single run.
  • The positive rate is a publication artefact. 72% of measured pairs are strong binders because inactive compounds are rarely published unless they sit in an active series.
  • This ranks candidates; it does not establish binding. No prediction here is evidence that any compound binds any target.
